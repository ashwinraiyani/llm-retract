# **Abstract** 

With the adoption of renewable energy in today's power network, the energy demand forecasting solutions become more required to be accurate, scalable and transparent. Existing forecasting methods tend to be concentrated on forecasting precision only and leave out model interpretability, which is a key challenging apply to vital energy infrastructure. Comparing the traditional method of renewable energy forecasting, this research suggests a new XAI based framework with cloud solutions for renewable energy demand forecasting. The framework comprises of machine learning models, deep learning models like Random Forest, XGBoost, Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) model, and hybrid ensemble model (HEM) along with Explainable AI techniques like SHAP and LIME. This scalable, Amazon S3-based cloud architecture uses SageMaker, Amazon lambda, API, and QuickSight to store data, deploy a model, push data to lambda, and generate decision-support visualization. Based on experimental results, the Hybrid Ensemble Model shows excellent forecasting performances that have the lowest forecasting errors and highest coefficient of determination (R² = 0.978). Analysing the explainability shows clear evidence of historical demand, renewable energy generation, temperature and solar irradiance being the most salient factors for energy demand. The proposed framework offers more transparency to the stakeholders of the utility and the policies, support in confidence building, and helps in regulatory compliance and informed decision making. Furthermore, the blueprint enables sustainable approaches to energy management to boost the utilisation of renewable energy, reduce energy waste, and foster climate action efforts. Based on the results it is possible to establish the feasibility of developing reliable and scalable renewable energy forecast system as future smart grid platforms from the synergies of AI, XAI and cloud computing. 

**Keywords** : Renewable Energy Demand Forecasting; Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI); Machine Learning; Deep Learning 

# **1. Introduction** 

# **1.1 Background** 

In the context of urgent efforts to achieve decarbonisation and combat climate change, the energy sector is going through an important transformation. In response to the need for sustainability and energy security, investment in renewable energy technologies, such as solar, wind, hydroelectric and biomass energy, is increasing as the governments, industries and international organisations seek to achieve the necessary targets. As renewable energy has established a stronghold in the present energy system and countries have ambitious targets for clean energy generation and carbon neutrality [1], numerous challenges have emerged in integrating renewable energy with existing power generation. With growing share of renewables — energy demand management and optimisation become key issues for utility companies and power grid operators. 

Energy demand forecasting plays an important role for efficient functioning of renewable energy systems. Modelers are used by stakeholders to forecast the demand for electricity and plan to ensure adequate resources are available as well as to strengthening the grid, improving its efficiency, and reducing electricity costs. Well prediction information of demand is extremely crucial for power production where they will take their decisions about generation of electricity according to the demand while keeping the wastage of energy minimum and increasing the energy efficiency of the whole process [2]. In a renewable energy situation the generation may be even less predictable, more variable and, to some extent, uncontrollable, increasing the tendency to risk prediction. 

The capabilities that Cloud Computing can bring to energy analytics systems have significantly been enhanced. Cloud platforms provide flexible computing platforms, and can be scaled up to large amounts of storage and powerful analytics services that can analyze data sizes from smart meters, sensors, Internet of Things (IoT) devices and weather stations. These platforms are used for offering real-time monitoring and predictive analytics, which assists the utility companies to make informed decisions about their operations [3]. With the use of artificial intelligence (AI) along with the cloudbased infrastructures, organizations can be able to create advanced forecasting systems for sustainable energy management and smart grid operation. 

# **1.2 Challenges in Renewable Energy Demand Prediction** 

Renewable energy demand forecast problem is still difficult although of all the advances projected in the technologies. One of the main problems is the variability in demand. Due to various economic, demographic and social behaviours, the electricity demand varies significantly among regions, seasons and also among the electricity consumers [4]. The variability makes it challenging to forecast more accurately, especially if the forecast is over the long-term. 

A significant issue is its reliance on climate for its economy. Environmental factors like solar irradiance, temperature, humidity, rainfall and wind speed, play an important role in renewable energy systems. These influence the electricity production as well as consumption and consequently the overall nonlinearity of this system makes it hard to predict in terms of the classical prediction techniques [5]. 

Uncertainty with regard to consumer behavior adds to the making of forecasts. Increased use of electric vehicles, ‘smart' appliances, a shift to distributed energy resources and the use of demandresponse programs are transforming energy usage. Such alterations introduce additional variables which are hard to model and predict accurately [6]. 

Moreover, many state-of-the-art AI methods for prediction, such as the deep learning prediction models, are black-box systems. These models tend to create a more accurate predictive model, but, gives little understanding as to the rationale behind their predictions. The lack of transparency may lead to lower stakeholder acceptance and lower trust of such AI based forecast solution in critical energy infrastructure [7]. 

# **1.3 Need for Explainable AI in Energy Systems** 

Predictive tools powered by Artificial Intelligence (AI) have surged in significance especially with the increased dependence on their use. Submissions that enhance the understanding of the decision making by these models and parses the XAI description of the model's behavior and results are especially welcome. These explanations can also aid energy managers, energy policy makers in 

interpreting, judging the outcomes of the forecasting and assess the behavior of the models and validate the models [7]. 

The ability to make accurate forecasts is especially critical to energy systems as forecast errors can result in economic losses, inefficient resource use and instability in the grid. An explainable model provides a built-in risk of error detection for decision-makers and enables assessments on if the predictions make sense in the domain and for operations. 

New regulations go along with the need for explainable AI solutions as well. There is no need to change the principles of new AI governance that have been presented above, which are accountability, transparency, fairness and responsible use of automated decision making systems [8]. Moreover, human-centred DSSs should be characterized by interpretable outputs, which helps the collaboration of AI technologies and humans experts. So, renewable energy forecasting model explainability has emerged as a key and ongoing research area. 

# **1.4 Cloud Platforms for Energy Analytics** 

Cloud computing platforms have emerged as a key ingredient in today's energy analytics. Their Scalability enables businesses to handle immense amounts of information without having to put in a lot of infrastructure. They provide the computing resources dynamically to the cloud according to the needs of the computations, so they can be used for and scaled to large forecasting applications [3]. Cloud also provides for real-time data processing, enabling to analyse the energy consumption/generation data in real time throughout. Such a capability is crucial in smart grid applications where critical forecasting information has to be available at the right time to improve operation efficiency and enable rapid and effective decisions. 

Furthermore, cloud platforms offer full services for the management of big data, such as data storage, integration, security and analytics. The Amazon Web Service (AWS) cloud ecosystem has been chosen as the basis for the proposed architecture in this study since it has a robust ecosystem of AWS machine learning services, AWS data processing services and AWS serverless computing services, all of which are relevant for renewable energy forecasting applications. 

# **1.5 Research Gap** 

While various studies have been undertaken for Renewable Energy Demand Forecasting (RRDF) using machine learning and deep learning methods, most of them have centered on enhancing the prediction accuracy leaving behind just a little consideration for model interpretability. There are many existing predictive models that are black-box models of Artificial Intelligence, that do not provide forers with a lot of insight on the prediction results. Furthermore, not many of the reported studies have integrated Explainable AI method in designs that are oriented towards cloud computing to ensure both real-time and scalable energy analysis. # **2. Literature Review** 

# **2.1 Renewable Energy Demand Forecasting** 

Motivated by the increasing penetration of renewable energy resources into today's electric power systems, renewable energy demand forecasting has become a crucial research field. Good forecasts are useful for energy supply and demand scheduling, demand side management, for grid stability and resource optimisation. From the prediction horizon usually the forecasting approaches can be classified as short term, medium term and long term forecasting [9]. 

# **Short-Term Forecasting** 

The typical period for short term forecasting is from a few minutes to a few days into the future, and is used directly for operational planning or for real time energy management. In tandem with smart meters and IoT devices, utilities now have the capacity to collect a higher frequency of data, to allow more accurate forecasts. By fitting these models to the nonlinear patterns in the demand and the shorttime fluctuations, the performance of machine learning models and deep learning models has been greater than conventional statistical models [10]. Weather variables, occupancy behavior and electricity pricing information, however, can be introduced effectively to achieve a more accurate short-term renewable energy demand prediction by adopting deep neural network or a hybrid forecast model [11]. 

# **Medium-Term Forecasting** 

Medium term (usually in range of few weeks to months) forecasting is for maintenance planning, energy trading & capacity allocation. Fusion of indicators economic, climatic and seasonal features to the case horizon forecasting is needed. Ensemble Learning approaches and hybrid AI models have been gaining attention by researchers for enhancing the reliability of forecasts on middle term horizons. A hybrid method which combines ensembled classifiers (Machine learning algorithms) and feature selection obtains better forecast results in various environments [12]. 

# **Long-Term Forecasting** 

Long term forecasting is an analysis of future demand outlook and is a forecast for several months/years. These forecasts are critical for planning, infrastructure and investments in renewable energy and for policy making. Long-term forecasting models often include social and economic, technological and population growth trends and take government policies into account. As of recently, it has been recommended that forecasting systems employing AI methods that can combine a wide variety of data sources to deliver greater confidence in long-term demand forecasting are of increasing importance [13]. 

# **2.2 Artificial Intelligence in Energy Forecasting** 

Artificial Intelligence is emerging as an innovative, new technology for energy demand forecasting. Relationships between several variables that are more complex and nonlinear can then be detected by AI techniques, which can adapt to different patterns in energy use. They have seen much attention to machine learning and deep learning in the last couple of years [14]. 

# **Machine Learning Models** 

Machine learning based methods are still in great use due to their promise of high predictive accuracy, at relatively low computational complexity. 

# **Random Forest** 

Random Forest (RF) is an ensemble method which constructs a number of trees and classifies the classes according to the output results of all of the trees to avoid a misjudgment. RF models are very good at working with high dimension energy data and are very useful in determining the key variables that are important in forecasting. RF models have been found to have better performance in renewable energy forecasting applications thanks to their capability to avoid overfitting [15]. 

# **XGBoost** 

Among various machine learning algorithms, Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) has emerged as a popularly used algorithm in energy forecasting. XGBoost provides a collection of gradient boosting algorithms which can correct the errors made by the prediction process and do this process in an iterative way until the accuracy comes closer to 100%. It has been shown effective in modelling such huge energy data sets, and works extremely well in handling the complex non-linear relationship between the weather variables and electricity demand [16]. 

# **Support Vector Machine (SVM)** 

With its excellent generalisation property, SVM still remains popular in energy forecast. SVM models have been considered to be very effective when the size of examples is small, and the space is not linearly separable. Many comparative studies have shown that SVM-based approach, combined with the optimization methods and features engineering can achieve competitive forecasting model performance. 

# **Deep Learning Models** 

Deep Learning combined with a sort of 'automatic learning of hierarchical representations' from large quantities of data is gaining popularity. 

# **Artificial Neural Networks (ANN)** 

Some of the first DL technologies used for energy prediction are Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs). ANNs are used to model non-linear relationships between the input variables, and the energy demand. They can be flexible and adaptable for usage in different forecasting applications with different renewable energy data [18]. 

# **Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)** 

LSTMs are a particular kind of Recurrent Neural Networks which are trained to pick up long-term temporal (sequence) dependency in the given data. LSTM models have shown to perform extremely well in renewable energy forecasting, which is crucial to process a time-series information and to account for temporal correlations. Many research papers have obtained remarkable results in forecast quality with the usage of architectures based on LSTM [19]. 

# **Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU)** 

GRU networks are more efficient from a computational perspective as compared to LSTMs. GRUs are more efficient at using the few parameters without impacting the forecasting performance. Recent studies drew the attention that, GRU models can be used for prediction because they have higher prediction accuracy while having lesser training time when they are applied to real time forecasting problems [20]. 

# **2.3 Cloud Computing for Smart Energy Systems** 

Given the ever increasing large and fast energy-related data, adoption of the so-called technologies of the “cloud” is rapidly being embraced when applied to the smart energy problem. Cloud structures provide scalable computing resources, and these can be used for advanced analytics, machine learning algorithms, real-time checking programs and so much more. 

# **Cloud-Based Energy Management** 

Distributed energy resources can be monitored and controlled on a broader scale with the use of cloud-based energy management systems. These enable the utility providers to collect, post process and analyse energy data from geographic energy sources. Smart energy environment had demonstrated the potential of cloud computing to increase operation efficiency, and resource harvesting, decision making [21]. 

# **Energy-as-a-Service Platforms** 

A new way of energy business model in the form of Energy-as-a-Service (EaaS), has emerged with Cloud technologies. Although EaaS platform does not require significant investment, it does let consumers and organisations access energy optimisation services. Modern EaaS systems consist of three basic services: cloud-based analytics, predictive maintenance and demand forecasting. 

# **Smart Grid Analytics** 

Smart grids generate and compile huge amounts of data from smart meters, sensors, and distributed generation. Cloud computing is required to process these flows of data, and extract actionable insights from them. The advantages of cloud deployments and performance make this approach to complex analytics, like Anomaly Detection, Loading Forecasting and Predictive Maintenance a popular choice [23]. 

# **2.4 Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) Concept and Importance** 

Explainable AI encompasses a range of methods and techniques for explaining, understanding and interpreting outputs of a machine learning model. With the growing complexity of AI models, XAI helps overcome their lack of transparency and can aid trusty decision-making process. In important applications like healthcare, finance, transportation, and energy, explainability is essential since decisions made can have great societal and economic implications [24]. 

# **Global XAI Trends** 

It is the subject of an enormous number of ideas and numerous works that started to be developed and inspired in the past few years in view of the growing challenges related to algorithmic accountability, fairness, transparency and compliance. There have been concerns among the global community and policy makers that the AI systems must be explainable and allow them to provide meaningful explanations for automated decisions. Therefore, when diving into AI deployment in numerous high-stake applications, explainability comes as a fundamental requirement [24]. 

# **XAI in Critical Infrastructure** 

AI systems in critical infrastructure need to be extremely reliable and transparent. Information about energy systems: Expressive quality helps stakeholders to understand the results of the forecast and to check if they see the correct behaviour of the models and if they understand their influence on the forecast. XAI techniques are also helpful in risk management and regulatory requirements by documentable processes in decision making [25]. 

# **2.5 Popular Explainability Methods** 

Over the years, a number of explanation methods have been developed to help gain a better understanding of machine learning models. 

# **SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations)** 

The idea of SHAP, which comes from the cooperative game theory, is to provide measures of the contribution of each feature to the model forecasts. SHAP offers local explanations as well as global explanation and is now among one of the most popular XAI method in energy forecasting applications [24]. 

# **LIME (Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations)** 

LIME models the behavior of any complex model locally, by trying to approximate complex model behavior at specific predictions. It will allow the stakeholders to see what lies behind individual forecasts made and what factors were important [24]. 

# **Permutation Feature Importance** 

Permutation Feature Importance: Computes the importance of a feature by measuring the drop in the predictive performance of the model after permuting the Data. This method gives a sort of “common sense” evaluation of the importance of a variable. 

# **Partial Dependence Plots** 

The Partial Dependence Plots illustrate the associations between the model inputs and output, while varying one of the model inputs and fixing the other. These plots are intended for stakeholders to have insight into what aspects of the individual features change forecasting results. 

# **Counterfactual Explanations** 

Counterfactual explanations: show the smallest modification of input variables needed to change the outcomes of the prediction. These explanations give actionable information and aid in the scenario analysis in decision making contexts. 

# **2.6 Existing XAI Applications in Energy Sector** 

The use of XAI in the energy field is yet relatively new yet gaining strong momentum. Examples of the use of the techniques in the field of energy are application of SHAP and LIME technique in electricity load forecasting and renewable energy generation prediction, smart grid optimization, and energy consumption analysis. Previous research shows that explainability helps gain trust from users or other stakeholders, to better validate models, and for regulatory compliance. However, most of the applications are for stand-alone forecasting systems, not in forecasting “ecosystems.” Besides this, there is a lack of studies that compared the different XAI methods in renewables forecasting, especially in the domain of renewable energy demand forecasting [25]. 

# **2.7 Research Gaps** 

Although there have been huge improvements in forecasting of renewables, there are still a number of research gaps. First, many forecasting research studies are directed towards the correct prediction of forecasts, while neglecting transparency and interpretability of forecast models. Second, from the literature review, it was found that there is very limited research work which applied these Explainable AI techniques in the renewable energy demand forecasting frameworks. Third, there are still behaviors arising at cloud level that need to be explored for scalable and real-time explainable forecasting systems. Finally, literature comparing explainable and non explainable methods for forecasting for RE applications is very scarce. Last but not least, there is a lack of complete framework implemented over the AWS, focusing on renewable energy demand prediction using machine learning, explainability, and the cloud. The present study aims to relate to these gaps as its major driver. 

# **3. Proposed Explainable AI Framework** 

The proposed system integrates the ideas of RENEW need forecasting, Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) and cloud computing, all in one system to make decisions in a scalable and transparent manner. The framework intends to gather energy-related data from several sources, and then process them and store in cloud using the machine learning models and forecast the energy demands, then provide explanations for the same with an explainable one. The desired use of the proposed architecture centers on allowing utility providers, grid operators or policy makers to easily manage renewable energy systems in an efficient manner in a transparent environment that can be scaled and is analytical in real-time. 

# **3.1 Overall System Architecture** 

Five intertwined layers build up the framework. 

# **_Layer 1: Data Acquisition Layer_** 

This layer pertains to the collection of data from smart meters, IoT based sensors, weather stations, renewable energy generation systems and utility databases. Data acquired are electricity usage data, data on solar irradiance, temperature, humidity, wind speed data and consumer behaviour data. 

# **_Layer 2: Cloud Storage Layer_** 

Data collected is securely transmitted to a cloud based data storage infrastructure for its secure storage & management. This layer facilitates the integration of data across a wide range and offers efficient access to data for analysis and model training. 

# **_Layer 3: AI Prediction Layer_** 

Machine learning and deep learning were applied to machine learning models such as Random Forest and XG Boost models and deep learning models such as LSTM and GRU models for prediction of 

renewable energy need. One such system is the prediction engine which pulls past historical pattern and real time data to make predictable forecasts. 

# **_Layer 4: Explainability Layer_** 

This layer explains SHAP and LIME explainer to explain forecasting results. Scale contribution analysis helps in engaging stakeholders in the understanding of the contributing factors to the results of prediction. 

# **_Layer 5: Decision Support Dashboard_** 

Visualisation of forecasts and results for explainability to be carried out using an interactive dashboard. The dashboard offers insightful operational data for energy planning, demand response and decision making. 

# **3.2 Workflow of the Proposed System** 

The workflow proposed is a sequential one: 

# **_Data Collection – Pre-processing – Cloud storage – AI Prediction – Explainability analysis – Decision dashboard_** 

Firstly, the data from various sources related to renewable energy and consumers are collected. Processed data (clean, normalized and feature engineered) are then stored in cloud environment. Demand forecasts are then produced using forecasting models which are trained and deployed. The contribution of each individual variable is then identified and the outputs of the models are analysed by the XAI techniques. Finally the outputs are supplied to a decision support dashboard for informative and transparent decisions. 

# **3.3 Cloud Platform Architecture (AWS-Based Deployment)** 

The suggested design uses Amazon Web Services (AWS) as one cloud platform to deploy on. AWS IoT Core is used to ingest IoT data and smart meter data, and to store them in Amazon S3 data lakes. Data integration and using AWS Glue for data transformation can be achieved, while the models can be trained, deployed and monitored with Amazon SageMaker. Real-time prediction services that are deployable to scalable prediction are deployed using AWS Lambda and API Gateway. Amazon's QuickSight visualization and dashboard building capabilities are used, and Amazon's forecast and explainability results are made available to the stakeholders in real time. 

# **3.4 Explainable Forecasting Pipeline** 

Energy data can be processed first to the historical data for consumption in forecast models and also for the real-time data for online forecasts. After predictions are made, SHAP values are computed for the contribution of the input variables at the global level and at the local level while LIME also gives the explanation at the instance level. The explanations generated are added to the dashboard which can then be used by the users to understand why a particular demand forecasts were generated. All of this brings AI-based energy management systems closer to regulations and helps to increase transparency and trust of stakeholders.

The most important variables that affect the renewable energy demand are identified based on the result of the correlation analysis, Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE), and feature importance measures.

# **4.4 Forecasting Models** 

Different forecasting models are developed along with their comparison and as a result the best model, capable of forecasting the renewable energy demand is obtained. 

# **Linear Regression** 

Linear Regression is a simple model that can be utilized to benchmark, baseline and compare the performance of forecasting models. 

# **Random Forest** 

Random Forest is selected as the method because of the following reasons: It reduces overfitting, it is applicable for high dimensional data, and it can capture the non-linear relationship. 

# **XGBoost** 

As XGBoost has better predictive power and is efficient in gradient boosting mechanism, it is used.

ANN models have been constructed to consider some nonlinear relationships between the weather, energy and consumer variables. 

# **LSTM**

# **4.7 Evaluation Metrics** 

Both, forecasting performance metric and explainability assessment metric are used to evaluate the proposed framework. 

# **1. Forecasting Metrics** 

# **Mean Absolute Error (MAE)** 

Uses the magnitude (without regard for direction) of prediction errors to measure. 

# **Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE)** 

Expresses the prediction accuracy in percentage of the real demands. 

# **Root Mean Square Error (RMSE)** 

Measures which predict the size of a forecast error and put more weight on bigger errors. 

# **Coefficient of Determination (R²)** 

Measures the degree of fit of the forecasting model. 

# **2. Explainability Metrics** 

# **Fidelity** 

Assesses accuracy of the underlying model explaining the behaviour to generate the forecast. 

# **Stability** 

Assesses the consistency of explanations seeing the presence of small changes in the input data. 

# **Consistency** 

Analyzes if there is a uniform explanation for similar predictions in situations. 

# **Interpretability Score** 

Determines the level of understanding of the explanations provided in the models and how they assist stakeholders to make decisions.

# **6.1 Understanding Forecast Results** 

The forecasting results reveal that the machine learning and deep learning approaches demonstrate an outstanding performance over traditional statistical approaches used for renewable energy demand forecasting. Based on the tabulated statistical results, the Hybrid Ensemble Model is the most accurate model, which has the least MAE, RMSE, MAPE value and the R² value of 0.978. This finding indicates that the fusion of the several different forecasting algorithms would boost the forecasting performance beneficial from the assets of individual algorithms and mitigate the drawbacks of individual algorithms [26]. 

The lower error of the model can be seen as superior performance of the ensemble model which was able to capture the nonlinear relationship that exists between weather, energy and consumer related variables. The deep learning approach model such as LSTM also exhibited a good prediction ability as it can capture the time-series data temporal combinations [27]. The findings are relevant because it indicates that more sophisticated AI approaches can be applied in single points to drastically enhance the accuracy in a renewable energy scenario with dynamically evolving demand, which involves multiple actors working interdependently with one another. 

**6.2 Key Drivers of Energy Demand** 

The explainability analysis revealed some very important factors with regard to the demand for renewables. 

# **Weather Effects** 

Weather factors were identified as playing a key role for the demand for energy. One of the largest contributions, as illustrated by SHAP, is that of temperature. An increase in demand for electricity was generally attributed to the increased cooling demand from residential and commercial buildings brought by higher temperatures [28]. Also snow reflectivity and solar irradiance had a strong positive influence because of their direct effect on the renewable energy generation capacity, but also in the indirect effect on the energy consumption pattern. The results of forecasting were not greatly affected and contributed by wind speed and rainfall under a certain environmental setting. 

# **Economic Effects** 

Another important factor for energy demand was identified as being the economic activity. Electric power consumption and industrial activity had a positive correlation which shows that industrial activity increased, the electricity consumption also increased. Geographical regions where economic development takes place are usually those where industry and infrastructure development, and hence electricity demand, is greater [29]. In turn, the findings validate other findings that indicated the role of economic indicators for medium and long term energy forecast. 

# **Consumer Behavior** 

Consumer variables such as density of population and household consumption did have an important influence on forecasting outcomes. The most important parameters found were related to consumption by households; as residential consumption is a large proportion of total, this figure was important. This complexity has been added to the consumer energy use because of the increase in smart appliances, EVs and distributed renewables [30]. Based on the results of this study, it is crucial to incorporate the behavioral and the demographic part in the prediction models.

For efficiently managing the use of energy resources and introducing clean energy technologies, it is useful to have appropriate forecasts. 

# **SDG 9: Industry, Innovation and Infrastructure** 

The fusion of AI, cloud and explainability to drive innovation for energy infrastructure. The framework will ensure the building of resilient, technically advanced energy systems that are able to meet future energy needs. 

# **SDG 11: Sustainable Cities** 

For the sustainable development of cities, smart cities need to be equipped with efficient energy management systems, too. The proposed design supports energy efficient smart energy planning, smart energy optimizations, and contributes towards sustainable city projects, being energy efficient. 

# **SDG 13: Climate Action** 

The framework directly contributes to the goals of climate action by enhancing the use of renewable energy and cutting carbon emissions. Forecasting tools using AI could accelerate India's journey towards its climate targets. 

# **9. Novel Contributions of the Study** 

It is one of the first studies to make contributions in innovative ways in the fields of renewable energy forecasting, Explainable Artificial Intelligence and cloud-based energy analytics. 

# **9.1 Cloud-based EXplicable Energy Forecasting Framework** 

The study posits an all-in-one approach of Renewable Energy Demand Forecasting, Cloud Computing and Explainable Artificial Intelligence under one roof. The proposed framework tackles three challenges together, namely, predictive performance, scalability and interpretability, which are not tackled by the conventional forecasting systems. 

# **9.2 Demand Prediction Model using Hybrid AI-XAI** 

To enhance the forecast accuracy, a novel Hybrid Ensemble Model which combines the machine learning and deep learning methods is constructed. By implementing XAI techniques such as SHAP and LIME, the decision-making process over the results of the forecasts is transparent and understandable to the users involved. 

# **9.3 Multi-Level Explainability Dashboard** 

The research suggests a multi-level explainability dashboard, offer both, the global and local level explainability. Global Explanations give the most significant forecasting variables and local Explanations give details on the forecasting results. 

# **9.4 Renewable Energy Decision Support System (REDS)** 

A full-fledged decision support system is also proposed, aiding the utility providers, the energy planner and the energy policy makers. This system is comprised of forecasting outcomes, insights on Explainability and cloud analytics for the decision making on operational and strategic levels. 

# **9.5 Explainability Score for Energy Forecasting Models** 

The notion of Explainability Score is introduced and it is proposed to test the outlooks provided by forecasting models based on this score with regards to four conditions: fidelity, stability, consistency and interpretability. This measure provides a new way to go beyond just predictive accuracy in forecasting evaluation and invites the creation of trustworthy Ai systems. 

# **9.6 Real-Time Cloud Analytics Architecture** 

Scalable AWS infrastructure for ingesting, forecasting and analysing data for explainability, and visualizing real time data. The design and analysis of the proposed architecture show the favorable 

integration of cloud native technologies in renewable energy management systems as a foundation to the smart energy infrastructures. 

Together these contributions make a significant leap forward in research in tackling the twofold goals of model transparency and forecast accuracy, and introduce the possibility of practical implementation of models through the use of cloud computing tools. 

# **10. Future Research Directions** 

While the framework proposed shows great promise, there are opportunities for further research and tech enhancements. 

# **10.1 Federated Learning for Privacy-Preserving Energy Forecasting** 

Future research is interested in studying federated learning approaches, where multiple energy providers can train forecasting model collaboratively without compromising the privacy of their consumer data. These strategies can contribute to privacy and regulatory compliance and model generalisation. 

# **10.2 Explainable Deep Reinforcement Learning in Smart Grids** 

Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) has the potential of being able to achieve autonomous decision making and dynamic energy optimisation. With incorporation of explainability approaches in DRL systems, it is believed that the transparency and trustworthiness of intelligent smart grid operations would be boosted. 

# **10.3 Digital Twin-Based Energy Management** 

Digital twin technologies offer the possibility of making virtual representations of energy systems that update as a more and more continuous flow of data offers. when paired with forecasting and explainability capabilities, their digital twins can help optimize predictive maintenance and operational optimization and facilitate scenario analysis. 

# **10.4 Generative AI for Energy Scenario Simulation** 

The generative AI models can be used to generate scenarios for future demand, policies, and renewable energy adoption. It is possible to use such simulations for long-term planning as well as strategic decision making in uncertain situations. 

# **10.5 Edge AI & Cloud integration** 

Edge computing will be growing in significance in the future, as well, processing data nearer to the generation and consumption source. Finally, Edge AI coupled with analytics in the cloud is a bigpicture strategy which could reduce latency, boost scalability and enable real-time energy management use cases. 

# **10.6 Energy Systems with Explanable Multi-agents** 

Multi-agent systems provide a great opportunity for the coordination of distributed energy resources, electric vehicles and smart devices. Future research can consider employing an explainable multiagent so that transparent coordination mechanisms and explainable decision making can be possible. These research directions can provide additional features of EEMS and help build reliable, scalable and viable renewable energy infrastructures. 

# **11. Conclusion** 

The framework for Renewable Energy (RE) load forecasting (RLF) guided by Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) is developed and can be used in the cloud. AWS Cloud services, explainability techniques, and machine learning and deep learning techniques are all applied to the development of a system that is scalable and transparent for the purposes of forecasting; this system could then be used to make intelligent solutions towards energy management. 

The results obtained showed that the results of forecasting using the models suited with advanced techniques, particularly the Hybrid Ensemble Model (HEM), can be more effective than the traditional models. The techniques of explainability, such as SHAP and LIME, have been able to successfully highlight the major factors affecting energy demand, thus benefiting the transparency and encouragement of stakeholders. The deployment architecture needed to store data, train models, 

forecast in real-time and visualize for decision-support purposes – and all of this in a scalable manner was delivered via AWS. 

The scientific findings of this study highlight the importance of explainability in critical infrastructure systems where the accuracy of their predictive decision-making can impact the efficiency of the system or decisions on resource allocations. Furthermore, the suggested model is able to accommodate the renewable energy planning such as renewable energy demand forecasting, management of the peak load, demand response programmes and integration of smart grid. 

The overall findings of the study demonstrate the significant potential impact AI, coupled with XAI and cloud computing, can have in managing renewable energy and how it can also contribute to sustainability and responsible usage of AI. The intelligent energy forecasting frameworks are likely to be even more effective and trustworthy with future advancements in federated learning (FL), digital twins, generative AI, and explainable multi-agent systems.
