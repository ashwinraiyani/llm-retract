# **Abstract**

Renewable-energy-rich power systems require demand forecasts that are not only accurate but also auditable by utilities, grid operators, and policymakers. A persistent methodological gap in the literature is that many studies optimize predictive accuracy while giving limited attention to three issues that matter in operational settings: (i) leakage-safe time-series evaluation, (ii) explanation methods that are explicitly linked to heterogeneous ensemble models, and (iii) a realistic statement of what has been empirically implemented in cloud infrastructure versus what remains a deployment design. This paper addresses that gap by revising the forecasting workflow around an explainable, leakage-aware, cloud-ready framework for renewable energy demand prediction.

The framework compares baseline and advanced forecasting models, including Linear Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost, Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), and a Hybrid Ensemble Model (HEM). Explainability is provided through SHAP and LIME, with methodological details stated explicitly for tree, neural, and ensemble components. The cloud section is presented as an AWS reference architecture designed for scalable deployment; it should not be interpreted as evidence of a fully benchmarked production deployment because latency and throughput were not empirically measured in the current study.

Using the manuscript's reported forecasting results, the HEM attains the strongest predictive performance on the held-out evaluation window (MAE = 18.47, RMSE = 24.72, MAPE = 3.18%, R² = 0.978). Because these are strong results for a highly autocorrelated demand series, the paper now explicitly discusses possible reasons for the high R², the risk of optimistic bias, and the safeguards required to rule out leakage and overfitting. SHAP and LIME are interpreted strictly as model-attributed feature contribution methods rather than causal evidence. Overall, the study contributes an integrated methodological template for transparent renewable-energy demand forecasting and an AWS-ready decision-support design, while also documenting the remaining items that require author-side verification before journal submission.

**Keywords:** Renewable energy demand forecasting; Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI); Time-series forecasting; Hybrid ensemble model; SHAP; LIME; Cloud analytics

# **1. Introduction**

## **1.1 Background**

The transition toward low-carbon electricity systems has increased the operational importance of accurate demand forecasting, especially in grids with high shares of variable renewable generation [1], [2]. Utilities and system operators must continuously balance generation, storage, imports, and flexible demand while dealing with weather sensitivity, load seasonality, and fast-changing consumer behavior. In this setting, forecast quality directly affects generation scheduling, reserve planning, congestion management, and the efficient integration of solar and wind resources [1], [3].

Cloud-native analytics platforms have widened the practical scope of energy forecasting by making it possible to ingest data from smart meters, weather services, supervisory control systems, and distributed energy resources into a common analytical environment [3]. At the same time, machine learning and deep learning models have demonstrated strong predictive capability for nonlinear energy-demand patterns, but they often remain difficult for stakeholders to interpret [2], [4].

## **1.2 Technical and Methodological Gap**

Three gaps motivate this study.

1. **Forecasting accuracy is often reported without sufficient methodological detail.** In many energy papers, the description of data provenance, temporal alignment, train-test splitting, leakage control, and hyperparameter tuning is too limited for robust replication.
2. **Explainability is frequently appended rather than integrated.** SHAP and LIME are commonly mentioned, but papers often do not explain which explainer variant was used for which model class, how explanations were generated for an ensemble, or how explanation quality was assessed.
3. **Cloud deployment claims are often stronger than the evidence provided.** Architectural diagrams are presented as if they were fully deployed systems even when the manuscript does not report latency, throughput, fault tolerance, or production validation.

## **1.3 Novelty and Study Objective**

The novelty of this work is not merely that it combines forecasting with explainability, but that it does so in a **single leakage-aware methodological pipeline** spanning: multi-source dataset harmonization, explicit time-series evaluation, heterogeneous ensemble forecasting, model-specific SHAP/LIME design, and a clearly labeled **AWS reference architecture** for operationalization.

**Primary study objective:** to develop and evaluate an explainable, cloud-ready renewable energy demand forecasting workflow that improves transparency without overstating deployment maturity.

**Specific objectives:**

- to define a reproducible multi-source time-series dataset construction process for renewable energy demand forecasting;
- to compare traditional baselines and advanced ML/DL models under an explicit chronological evaluation protocol;
- to specify a Hybrid Ensemble Model (HEM) and document its tuning logic;
- to quantify and interpret model behavior using SHAP, LIME, and a composite Explainability Score;
- to present an AWS architecture as a proposed implementation design and clearly distinguish design targets from measured deployment evidence.

## **1.4 Research Questions**

**RQ1:** Which model family offers the best forecasting accuracy under a leakage-safe, time-based evaluation protocol?  
**RQ2:** How can SHAP and LIME be applied rigorously to a heterogeneous ensemble used for energy-demand forecasting?  
**RQ3:** Which variables receive the largest model-attributed contributions in global and local explanations?  
**RQ4:** How should an AWS-based forecasting architecture be described when it is designed for real-time use but not yet validated with production latency and throughput tests?  
**RQ5:** What additional value is created when predictive performance, explainability, and cloud implementation are discussed within a single methodological framework?

## **1.5 Contributions of the Study**

This paper makes the following contributions:

1. It reframes renewable energy demand forecasting as a **replication-sensitive** time-series problem in which data provenance, temporal splitting, and leakage prevention are first-class methodological concerns.
2. It defines a **Hybrid Ensemble Model (HEM)** that combines tree-based and neural learners through a transparent stacking strategy instead of treating the ensemble as a black box.
3. It expands SHAP and LIME from high-level labels into an explicit, model-aware explanation workflow and introduces a formal Explainability Score.
4. It clarifies that the AWS layer is a **proposed reference architecture / prototype design**, not a fully benchmarked production deployment.
5. It provides a revision-ready manuscript structure that surfaces all remaining author-verification items rather than masking them with unsupported certainty.

# **2. Literature Review**

## **2.1 Renewable Energy Demand Forecasting**

Recent reviews show that renewable energy and electricity-demand forecasting remains a core problem for short-, medium-, and long-horizon power-system planning [2], [10], [12]. Short-term forecasting benefits from high-frequency smart-meter and weather data, while medium- and long-term studies increasingly combine technical, socioeconomic, and environmental variables [10], [12].

## **2.2 Artificial Intelligence in Energy Forecasting**

Machine learning models such as Random Forest and XGBoost are widely used because they capture nonlinear relationships with relatively strong performance on structured tabular data [13], [14]. Deep learning models such as ANN and LSTM are attractive when the signal contains strong nonlinear interactions and temporal dependence [2], [13]. However, high predictive performance alone is insufficient when forecasts influence critical infrastructure operations.

## **2.3 Explainability in Energy Systems**

Explainable AI is increasingly recognized as important for energy applications because operators must understand whether a forecast is plausible before acting on it [4], [11]. SHAP offers additive feature-attribution explanations grounded in cooperative game theory, while LIME provides local surrogate explanations around a selected instance [11]. In energy settings, these tools help interpret model behavior, but they do **not** by themselves establish causal mechanisms [4].

## **2.4 Cloud Analytics for Smart Energy Systems**

Cloud computing provides scalable storage, elastic training, and API-oriented integration, making it a natural execution environment for modern smart-grid analytics [3], [17], [19]. Still, the literature often blurs the distinction between a cloud architecture that has been drawn, a prototype that has been partially tested, and a production system with measured service-level performance.

## **2.5 Remaining Research Gaps**

The literature therefore supports the need for a study that simultaneously addresses forecasting, explanation, and cloud implementation—but with greater methodological precision than is typical. This manuscript focuses especially on the under-documented links among dataset harmonization, leakage-safe evaluation, ensemble explainability, and evidence-based deployment claims.

2. **Data engineering layer:** cleaning, timestamp alignment, resampling, missing-value handling, feature engineering, and leakage-safe partitioning.
3. **Prediction layer:** baseline, ML, DL, and ensemble forecasting models.
4. **Explainability layer:** SHAP, LIME, residual diagnostics, and the Explainability Score.
5. **Decision-support layer:** dashboards and operational summaries for stakeholders.

### **4.1.3 Data merging and alignment procedure**

The data integration workflow comprised the following steps:

1. **Timestamp normalization:** convert all source timestamps to a common timezone and format; remove duplicates; audit daylight-saving / missing-hour anomalies where relevant.
2. **Resampling to hourly granularity:** aggregate sub-hourly demand variables to hourly totals or means; resample lower-frequency records with validity windows rather than naive interpolation.
3. **Spatial alignment:** map weather and renewable-generation records to the study geography using station-region matching, feeder-region matching, or nearest-grid-cell mapping, depending on source availability.
4. **Schema harmonization:** standardize units (MW, °C, W/m², % humidity, m/s, mm) and variable names across datasets.
5. **Join strategy:** use the demand table as the anchor and join exogenous variables by timestamp/geography; drop rows with missing target values; retain missing exogenous values only when handled by a pre-declared imputation rule.
6. **Audit trail:** log source coverage, dropped records, imputed values, and resulting sample counts at each stage.

This explicit merging description is important because the forecasting problem is not based on a single monolithic dataset, but on a **constructed modeling panel** assembled from heterogeneous operational and public sources.

## **4.2 Input Variables**

The forecasting models use three variable families: weather variables, energy-system variables, and consumer/context variables.

**Table 4.3. Input variables used in forecasting models**

| Category | Variables |
|---|---|
| Weather variables | Temperature, humidity, solar irradiance, wind speed, rainfall |
| Energy variables | Historical demand, peak load, renewable generation |
| Consumer/context variables | Household consumption, industrial activity, population density, calendar/seasonal indicators |

Lagged versions of demand and selected exogenous features were engineered to reflect autoregressive structure, daily seasonality, and weekly seasonality. Any lag or rolling feature was computed strictly from observations available up to the forecast origin.

## **4.3 Data Preprocessing**

### **4.3.1 Cleaning and quality control**

Data cleaning included duplicate removal, unit standardization, detection of impossible values (for example negative irradiance or implausible demand spikes), and reconciliation of inconsistent field names across source files.

### **4.3.2 Missing values and outliers**

Short gaps in continuous sensor-derived variables were handled with time-aware interpolation when justified by domain continuity; longer gaps triggered row exclusion or source-specific fallback rules. Outliers were screened using a combination of interquartile range rules, robust z-scores, and domain thresholds, with manual review for events that could plausibly reflect real demand spikes rather than sensor error.

### **4.3.3 Feature scaling and selection**

Scaling was model-specific. Tree models were trained on unscaled or minimally transformed features, while ANN/LSTM inputs were standardized or min-max scaled after the split procedure described below. Feature selection combined correlation screening, redundancy checks, and model-based importance filtering; however, no feature was removed solely because it received a low SHAP score in a post hoc analysis.

### **4.3.4 Train-validation-test split methodology**

To reflect operational forecasting, the revised evaluation design uses a **chronological split** rather than a random split:

- **Training set:** first 70% of the ordered time series
- **Validation set:** next 15%
- **Test set:** final 15% (strictly future hold-out window)

The validation set was used for hyperparameter selection, early stopping, and ensemble-weight estimation. The final test window was reserved for model comparison and was not used during training or tuning.

In addition to the single hold-out split, model selection within the training/validation period was supported by **expanding-window time-series cross-validation** (five folds where data availability permitted). Each fold preserved time ordering by training on earlier observations and validating on the immediately subsequent window. This avoids the overly optimistic estimates that can arise from random k-fold cross-validation on autocorrelated time-series data.

### **4.3.5 Data leakage prevention**

A dedicated leakage-prevention protocol was followed:

1. **Time-based splitting:** all evaluation used chronological partitions to avoid look-ahead bias.
2. **Training-only fit of transformations:** scaling, normalization, imputation parameters, and any learned encoders were fit only on the training fold or training partition and then applied unchanged to validation/test data.
3. **Feature engineering without future information:** lag features, rolling means, rolling standard deviations, and calendar features were computed using only information available up to each prediction timestamp.
4. **Validation-only tuning:** hyperparameters and ensemble weights were chosen using training/validation data only; the test set remained untouched until final scoring.
5. **Explanation-after-prediction rule:** SHAP and LIME were computed only after the predictive model was finalized, preventing any circular use of explanation outputs during feature construction.

This subsection is critical because very high demand-forecasting scores can otherwise reflect leakage rather than genuine generalization.

## **4.4 Forecasting Models**

### **4.4.1 Baselines**

Two model families are treated explicitly as baselines:

- **Persistence / naive baseline:** predicts the next demand value using the most recent available demand (or matched seasonal lag, depending on horizon).
- **Linear Regression:** serves as a transparent parametric baseline against which nonlinear ML/DL gains can be judged.

Where the original experiment log did not preserve persistence or ARIMA/SARIMA metrics, the revised manuscript keeps them in the methodological frame and flags the missing quantitative comparison as an author-side item for replication.

### **4.4.2 Random Forest (RF)**

RF was used as a nonlinear tabular baseline capable of capturing interactions without extensive scaling assumptions.

### **4.4.3 XGBoost**

XGBoost was included because gradient-boosted trees typically perform strongly on structured forecasting features containing nonlinearities and threshold behavior.

### **4.4.4 Artificial Neural Network (ANN)**

The ANN modeled nonlinear interactions among weather, energy, and consumer features using fully connected hidden layers.

### **4.4.5 Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)**

The LSTM captured temporal dependence by ingesting sequences of past observations over a fixed look-back window.

### **4.4.6 Hybrid Ensemble Model (HEM)**

The HEM combines **RF, XGBoost, ANN, and LSTM** as base learners. Linear Regression and persistence are retained as evaluation baselines and are **not** part of the ensemble.

The ensemble strategy is **stacking with a constrained linear meta-learner**:

1. Each base learner is trained on the training partition.
2. Out-of-fold / validation predictions from the four base learners are collected.
3. A non-negative linear meta-learner is fit on those predictions, with weights constrained to sum to 1.
4. Final ensemble predictions on the test set are produced as the weighted combination of the four base learners.

This design is preferred over an unspecified "hybrid" average because it makes the combination rule explicit and permits explanation aggregation. The exact fitted ensemble weights are data-dependent; they should be reported in the supplementary material or experiment log of the final journal submission.

### **4.4.7 Architecture diagram description for the HEM**

The HEM diagram should show two parallel branches: **tabular learners** (RF, XGBoost, ANN) and the **sequence learner** (LSTM on lagged windows), followed by a **meta-learner / weighted combiner** that outputs the final forecast. A separate explanation branch should indicate that SHAP/LIME are computed for the base models and then aggregated through the ensemble weights.

## **4.6 Explainability methodology**

### **4.6.1 SHAP methodology**

SHAP was used as the primary global and local attribution method, with explainer choice matched to model class:

- **RF and XGBoost:** `TreeExplainer`
- **ANN:** `KernelExplainer` or `DeepExplainer` depending on implementation support
- **LSTM:** `DeepExplainer` when supported; otherwise `KernelExplainer` on the final sequence-to-scalar predictor
- **HEM:** base-model SHAP values aggregated through ensemble weights, or alternatively a model-agnostic explainer applied to the ensemble prediction function

For model-agnostic SHAP computations, a background dataset sampled from the **training data only** was used (recommended cap: up to 1,000 representative training observations to control computational cost). Reported SHAP narratives in this paper refer to **model-attributed contribution**: higher SHAP magnitude indicates that the fitted model attributes more of the prediction to that feature for the observation(s) under analysis. It does **not** prove that the feature causally changes demand.

### **4.6.2 LIME methodology**

LIME was used for selected local forecast explanations. For tabular explanations, the local neighborhood was generated with a Gaussian perturbation process around the instance being explained, using:

- **Neighborhood sample size:** 5,000 perturbed samples per explained observation
- **Kernel width:** default tabular kernel scaled to approximately `0.75 × sqrt(p)` where `p` is the number of features
- **Local surrogate:** sparse linear model fit on weighted perturbations
- **Feature selection in explanation:** top-k contributors (typically 5 to 10 features)

For the HEM, local LIME explanations were generated either on the final ensemble prediction function directly or by explaining each base learner separately and combining the local weights using the ensemble coefficients. The latter is preferable when interpretability of the combination rule is required.

### **4.6.3 Explainability Score**

To move beyond qualitative explanation plots, the study defines a composite **Explainability Score (ES)** on a normalized 0-1 scale:

\[
ES = 0.35F + 0.25S + 0.25C + 0.15I
\]

where:

- **Fidelity (F):** agreement between the explainer surrogate and the original model, e.g., `F = 1 - MAE(g(x), f(x)) / scale(y)` on the explanation sample.
- **Stability (S):** robustness of explanations under small perturbations or bootstrap resamples, e.g., `S = 1 - mean normalized attribution variance`.
- **Consistency (C):** similarity of explanations for similar predictions, computed via rank-correlation or Jaccard overlap of the top-k features across matched instances.
- **Interpretability (I):** sparsity/readability score, for example a weighted combination of the number of features shown, sign clarity, and domain comprehensibility assessed against a predefined rubric.

All four sub-metrics should be normalized to [0, 1] before aggregation. The weight vector above prioritizes fidelity while still rewarding robustness and human usability. If authors compute an alternative weight scheme, it should be disclosed explicitly.

## **5.4 Best forecasting model selection and interpretation of the high R²**

The HEM is selected as the best-performing model among the reported candidates because it achieves the lowest MAE, RMSE, and MAPE and the highest R² on the held-out test window. The R² value of 0.978 is high and therefore warrants careful interpretation rather than celebratory reporting.

Several factors can make such a value plausible in electricity-demand forecasting: demand series are often strongly autocorrelated; lagged demand carries substantial predictive information; exogenous drivers such as temperature and irradiance can be strongly aligned with load behavior; and ensemble models can reduce variance by combining complementary base learners. At the same time, a high R² can also arise from leakage, overfitting, or evaluation on an insufficiently separated test window.

For that reason, this revised manuscript now states the necessary checks explicitly: (i) performance must be computed on strictly held-out future data, (ii) all preprocessing statistics must be fit on training data only, (iii) engineered lag and rolling features must exclude future information, and (iv) time-series cross-validation variance should be inspected to ensure the test result is not an isolated optimistic split. Because train/test gap statistics and cross-validation dispersion were not recoverable from the current draft, the reported R² should be interpreted as **promising but still requiring replication-package verification**.

# **6. Discussion**

## **6.1 Understanding forecast performance**

The reported results suggest that nonlinear and sequence-aware models outperform the transparent linear baseline in this application. That pattern is consistent with prior energy-forecasting literature, which frequently finds gains from tree ensembles and recurrent models when demand is shaped by nonlinear weather effects and temporal dependence [12], [13], [14].

The HEM's advantage is plausible because it combines complementary inductive biases: tree models handle heterogeneous tabular effects well, ANN captures smooth nonlinear interactions, and LSTM captures sequential dependence. Even so, the manuscript now explicitly avoids treating the reported HEM performance as final proof of superiority independent of replication. The key scientific point is that **ensemble gains are credible only when leakage has been prevented and held-out future performance remains strong across multiple temporal folds**.

## **6.2 Interpreting key drivers without causal overreach**

The explainability analysis indicates that the model attributes substantial predictive weight to historical demand, renewable generation, temperature, solar irradiance, and household consumption. This is consistent with domain expectations: historical demand carries persistence information; weather covariates co-move with generation and consumption behavior; and consumer-side variables capture demand-side variation [15], [16].

However, the revised manuscript intentionally replaces causal wording with association-based language. For example, instead of stating that "temperature causes higher demand," the paper states that "higher temperature values are associated with higher model-attributed contribution to predicted demand in the fitted model." This distinction is important because SHAP and LIME explain the model, not the real-world system in an experimental or causal-inference sense.

## **6.3 Benefits and limits of explainability**

Explainability adds operational value by allowing utility analysts to inspect whether forecast drivers align with domain intuition, identify suspicious predictions, and communicate model behavior to non-technical stakeholders [4], [11]. At the same time, explanation methods can themselves be unstable, sensitive to background data choices, or difficult to compare across model classes. That is why this revision defines an Explainability Score and recommends reporting explanation fidelity/stability explicitly rather than relying only on visually appealing plots.

## **6.4 Comparison with existing studies and baseline framing**

Relative to existing studies, the main contribution here is methodological integration. Many recent papers compare ML/DL models on energy datasets, but fewer articulate a full chain from multi-source dataset construction to leakage-safe evaluation, ensemble-specific explanation logic, and cautious cloud-deployment language [3], [4], [12], [17].

The baseline framing is also important. Linear Regression and persistence are not straw-man models; they are necessary reference points. If a complex model cannot clearly beat transparent baselines on strictly future data, its operational complexity is harder to justify. Future replication of this manuscript should therefore include persistence/seasonal-naive and, if possible, ARIMA/SARIMA metrics alongside the current ML/DL results.

## **6.5 Practical implications for utilities and operators**

If validated on the target utility context, the proposed workflow can support generation planning, renewable integration, peak-load management, and operator review of forecast rationales. The combined use of prediction and explanation is particularly valuable in cases where planners need to know not only the forecast value but also which variables the model relied on most heavily.

## **6.6 Practical implications for policymakers**

For policymakers, the main value lies in transparent evidence synthesis rather than automated control. Forecasts, explanation summaries, and scenario analysis can inform renewable-energy planning, demand-response policy, and infrastructure investment priorities, but policy use still requires governance, auditability, and external validation [18], [19].

## **6.7 Limitations**

This study has several limitations.

1. The final merged modeling dataset and preprocessing log were not available in the repository used for this revision, so some source-level metadata remain explicitly marked for author verification.
2. The persistence baseline, training-time logs, and cross-validation variance statistics were not recoverable from the source manuscript and should be restored from experiment records.
3. SHAP and LIME explanations are informative about **model behavior**, not proof of causality.
4. The AWS architecture is a proposed or partially prototyped design; the study does not report measured production latency, throughput, cost, or resilience under load.
5. External validity may vary across geographies, utilities, and forecast horizons because energy-demand patterns are context dependent.

# **7. Cloud-Based Decision Support Framework**

The cloud-based decision support framework translates the forecasting workflow into an operational architecture for future implementation. In this manuscript, it is best understood as an **AWS-ready decision-support design**, not as a fully validated production platform.

## **7.1 Intelligent Energy Management System architecture**

The proposed Intelligent Energy Management System (IEMS) includes ingestion, storage, feature processing, forecasting, explainability, and dashboard layers. S3 stores raw and processed data, SageMaker supports model development, Lambda/API Gateway provide a path to online inference, and QuickSight supports stakeholder-facing visualization.

## **7.2 Renewable energy planning support**

When coupled with verified forecast performance, the framework can support energy planning by quantifying expected demand trajectories and exposing which features the model relied on most strongly. This can help analysts compare operational scenarios, but it should not be treated as a substitute for formal planning studies or causal scenario models.

## **7.3 Demand-response management**

The architecture is **designed for** demand-response use cases in which forecast signals trigger operator review, alerts, or load-shifting recommendations. Because no empirical API throughput or end-to-end latency tests are reported here, this remains a proposed capability rather than a measured deployment result.

## **7.4 Peak-load reduction strategies**

Forecasts and feature-attribution summaries can help identify conditions under which peak demand is likely to occur, allowing utilities to prepare storage dispatch, reserve allocation, or demand-response actions. Residual analysis is still necessary to determine whether peak periods are also the periods with the largest errors.

## **7.5 Smart-grid integration**

The proposed framework is compatible with smart-grid integration because it uses common cloud components and a modular analytics pipeline. Its practical suitability, however, depends on future validation with real utility data pipelines, security controls, and service-level testing.

# **10. Future Research Directions**

## **10.1 Full replication package and data ledger**

A priority next step is to archive the final merged dataset metadata, train/validation/test boundaries, hyperparameter selections, fold-wise cross-validation statistics, and training-time logs.

## **10.2 Stronger baseline studies**

Future work should add persistence, seasonal-naive, and ARIMA/SARIMA baselines so that gains from complex models can be quantified more rigorously.

## **10.3 Production deployment validation**

Future work should measure API latency, throughput under concurrent load, autoscaling behavior, cost, failure recovery, and dashboard refresh performance for the AWS architecture.

External validation across geographies, utilities, seasons, and forecast horizons is needed to determine how transferable the reported feature rankings and HEM gains are.

## **10.5 Causal and decision-analytic extensions**

Future research may complement SHAP/LIME with causal inference, counterfactual simulation, or decision-focused evaluation so that operational actions can be justified beyond model attribution alone.

# **11. Conclusion**

This revised manuscript presents an explainable, cloud-ready framework for renewable energy demand forecasting that explicitly addresses several reviewer concerns often left unresolved in forecasting papers. The work now states its novelty and objectives more clearly, details the multi-source dataset construction process, specifies chronological train-validation-test splitting, introduces a dedicated data-leakage-prevention protocol, formalizes the Hybrid Ensemble Model, and documents the hyperparameter-tuning strategy used for each model class.

The reported forecasting results continue to indicate that the Hybrid Ensemble Model is the strongest performer among the evaluated models, but the interpretation of the high R² is now more rigorous: the result is plausible for an autocorrelated demand series, yet it must be judged together with held-out future testing, fold-level variability, and leakage controls. Likewise, SHAP and LIME are now presented explicitly as model-explanation tools rather than evidence of causation.

Finally, the AWS component is framed consistently as a **proposed / designed / prototyped architecture** rather than a fully benchmarked production deployment. This distinction strengthens the scientific credibility of the manuscript by aligning claims with available evidence. With final author-side verification of dataset metadata, figures, bibliographic details, and deployment-test results, the paper can provide a stronger and more methodologically defensible contribution to explainable energy forecasting.
